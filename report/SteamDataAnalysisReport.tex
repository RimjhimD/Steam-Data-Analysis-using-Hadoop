\section{Problem Statement}

Analyse the Steam game-recommendations dataset with Apache Hadoop (MapReduce) and Apache Pig,
across its game and review tables, to find which games and rating groups draw the most reviews and
the most hours of play on the Steam platform. The work follows the course brief:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Three MapReduce programs in Java}, each built on a different design pattern —
        summarization, data organization (binning) and a reduce-side join.
  \item \textbf{Five Apache Pig Latin scripts} for ranking and grouping questions on the same data,
        including a map-side replicated join over the full 41-million-row review table.
\end{itemize}

Everything was run on a single-node Hadoop cluster, and every job result in this report was read back
out of HDFS after the job that produced it had finished.

\section{Map Reduce Analysis}

\subsection{Review Summary per Game — Summarization}
\textbf{Returns, for every game, how many reviews it received, how many recommend it, how long its
reviewers played it and how many helpful votes its reviews collected} (\code{GameReviewSummary}).

This is the \textbf{numerical summarization} pattern. The mapper emits the game's \code{app\_id} with a
small tuple — a count of one, a 1 or 0 for ``recommended'', the hours played and the helpful votes.
Every field is additive (a count, a sum or a maximum), so partial results can be merged in any order;
that is what makes a \textbf{combiner} valid, and the reducer finishes with exactly the same code.
The analysis consists of a \textbf{Driver} (registers the reducer as the combiner too), a
\textbf{Mapper} (emits \code{app\_id} and a \code{ReviewStatsTuple}), a \textbf{Reducer} (merges all
tuples of one game; also the combiner) and a \textbf{Tuple} class (the custom \code{Writable}).

\subsection{Binning Games by Rating — Data Organization}
\textbf{Performs binning on the Steam rating label, writing every game into one output file per
bin.} The program is \code{BinningByRating}.

This is the \textbf{binning} pattern. The job is map-only: each mapper decides which bin a record
belongs to and writes it straight to that bin's file through \code{MultipleOutputs} — no shuffle, no
sort, no reducer. The bin name is the \code{rating} with spaces removed (``Very Positive'' becomes
\code{VeryPositive-m-00000}), a counter per bin records how many games landed in it, and
\code{LazyOutputFormat} stops Hadoop writing an empty default \code{part-m-00000}. The analysis
consists of a \textbf{Driver} (zero reducers, registers the \code{bins} named output) and a
\textbf{Mapper} (writes each game to its bin and counts it).

\subsection{Games Joined with their Reviews — Reduce-side Join}
\textbf{Returns each game's title and rating together with how many people reviewed it and how many
hours they played it} (\code{GameReviewJoin}).

This is a \textbf{reduce-side join}. The two inputs have different layouts, so each gets its own mapper
through \code{MultipleInputs}; both key on \code{app\_id} and tag their values — \code{G} for a game,
\code{R} for review data — so the reducer, which receives everything for one \code{app\_id} together,
can put the two sides back together. Sending 41 million reviews through the shuffle one by one would
be slow, so the review mapper uses \textbf{in-mapper combining}: a running total per game in a hash map,
emitted once per game when its split is done. The join is inner — games nobody reviewed, and reviews
whose game is missing, are dropped and counted. The analysis consists of a \textbf{Driver}, a
\textbf{Game Mapper}, a \textbf{Recommendation Mapper} and a \textbf{Join Reducer}.

\section{Pig Analysis}

All five scripts read the dataset from HDFS with \code{PigStorage(',')} and drop the header row with
\code{FILTER \dots\ BY app\_id IS NOT NULL} (the header's ``app\_id'' fails the cast to \code{int} and
becomes null — which is what the \code{FIELD\_DISCARDED\_\allowbreak TYPE\_\allowbreak CONVERSION\_\allowbreak FAILED} warning at the end of
each run counts). Results are stored tab separated under \code{/steam/pig/}; a tab rather than a pipe,
because 104 game titles contain a \code{|}. Every ranking sorts inside a nested \code{FOREACH}
(\code{ORDER} then \code{LIMIT}) rather than with a top-level \code{ORDER BY}: for the ``in each rating
group'' queries that is what the question needs, and for the overall rankings it avoids the sampling
job a top-level \code{ORDER BY} starts.

\subsection{Top 5 Rating Groups}
\textbf{Returns the five Steam rating labels that the most games carry.} Games are grouped by
\code{rating} and counted; the counts are gathered into one group (\code{GROUP \dots\ ALL}) so they can be
sorted together and cut to five. Pig compiles this into two MapReduce jobs — the count (with a
combiner) and the ranking.

\subsection{Top 10 Games by Total Hours Played}
\textbf{Returns the ten games whose reviewers played them for the most hours in total.} This script reads
the full 2 GB review table: it adds up the hours per game with \code{GROUP BY app\_id} and \code{SUM},
then brings in each game's title and rating with a \textbf{replicated join} — \code{games.csv} is small
enough to be copied to every mapper, so the join happens on the map side without a second shuffle.
The hours stay numeric for the sort and are formatted with \code{SPRINTF} only for output. Pig runs it
as a chain of three MapReduce jobs: a map-only job that prepares \code{games.csv} for the replicated
join, one with 15 maps and 3 reducers that groups the 41 million reviews and joins, and a final
ranking job.

\section{What the Data Says}

\textbf{Steam reviews are mostly positive.} 85.8\% of the 41 million reviews recommend the game, and
71.7\% of all games carry one of the four positive labels. Only 2,226 games (4.4\%) carry a negative one,
and just 14 are Overwhelmingly Negative.

\textbf{Attention is concentrated in a few games.} Of the 50,872 games, 37,610 appear in the review data
at all, and half of those have 39 reviews or fewer. The ten most reviewed games — 0.03\% of the reviewed
games — hold 5.4\% of all reviews.

\textbf{The most played games are long-running multiplayer titles.} Team Fortress 2, Counter-Strike:
Global Offensive, Dota 2 and Rust each passed 90 million hours of play among their reviewers, and the
average Counter-Strike or Dota 2 reviewer had played about 429 hours. Across all reviews the players had
logged 4.14 billion hours.

\textbf{Popular does not mean well rated.} PUBG: BATTLEGROUNDS is the second most reviewed game on Steam
but is rated Mixed, with 57\% positive reviews; Overwatch® 2 has 181,198 reviews and is Overwhelmingly
Negative. At the other end, Wallpaper Engine is 98.2\% positive in the review data.

\textbf{A rating label is partly a volume label.} The Positive and Negative groups top out at 49 reviews,
because Steam reserves the ``Very'' and ``Overwhelmingly'' labels for games with more reviews. Grouping by
\code{rating} therefore mixes how good the reviews are with how many there are — worth keeping in mind
before comparing the groups directly.

\section{Conclusion}

A 2.2 GB, three-table Steam dataset was loaded into HDFS and analysed on a single-node Hadoop 3.4.1
cluster with three Java MapReduce programs and five Pig Latin scripts, all of which completed
successfully. The summarization job shows how a custom \code{Writable} and a combiner shrink 41 million
map outputs to under a hundred thousand before the shuffle; the binning job shows a map-only job
writing many outputs at once; and the reduce-side join shows two differently shaped inputs meeting in
one reducer, with in-mapper combining making the join over 41 million rows finish in just over a
minute. Pig answered the ranking questions in 15 to 33 lines per script, including a replicated join
over the full review table, but took roughly three to four times as long as the hand-tuned Java join
for the same answer. The two tools agreed on every result they share, which is the strongest evidence
that both are correct.

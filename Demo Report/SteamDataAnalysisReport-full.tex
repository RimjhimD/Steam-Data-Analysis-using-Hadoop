\section{Problem Statement}

Analyse the Steam game-recommendations dataset with Apache Hadoop (MapReduce) and Apache Pig,
across its game and review tables, to find which games and rating groups draw the most reviews and
the most hours of play on the Steam platform.

The work is split the way the course asks for it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Three MapReduce programs in Java}, each built on a different MapReduce design
        pattern — summarization, data organization (binning) and a reduce-side join.
  \item \textbf{Five Apache Pig Latin scripts} that ask ranking and grouping questions of the same
        data, including a map-side replicated join over the full 41-million-row review table.
\end{itemize}

Everything was run on a single-node Hadoop cluster, and every job result in this report was read back
out of HDFS after the job that produced it had finished.

\section{Summary of Data Analysis}

The following MapReduce design patterns are implemented:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Summarization} — numerical summarization with a custom \code{Writable} and a combiner.
  \item \textbf{Data Organization} — binning with \code{MultipleOutputs}.
  \item \textbf{Join Patterns} — reduce-side join with \code{MultipleInputs}.
\end{enumerate}

The following MapReduce analyses are performed on the dataset:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Review summary per game} — number of reviews, recommended share, total, average and
        maximum hours played, and helpful votes for every game (\emph{summarization}).
  \item \textbf{Binning games by rating} — every game written to one of nine output files, one per
        Steam rating label (\emph{data organization}).
  \item \textbf{Games joined with their reviews} — each game's title and rating put next to its review
        count and total hours played (\emph{reduce-side join}).
\end{enumerate}

The following Pig analyses are performed on the dataset:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Top 5 rating groups by number of games
  \item Top 10 most reviewed games
  \item Top 10 most reviewed games in each rating group
  \item Top 10 games by total hours played
  \item Top 10 games by total hours played in each rating group
\end{enumerate}

\section{Map Reduce Analysis}

\subsection{Review Summary per Game}
\textbf{Returns, for every game, how many reviews it received, how many recommend it, how long its
reviewers played it and how many helpful votes its reviews collected} (\code{GameReviewSummary}).

This is the \textbf{numerical summarization} pattern. The mapper reads one review at a time and emits
the game's \code{app\_id} with a small tuple holding a count of one, a 1 or 0 for ``recommended'',
the hours played and the helpful votes. Every field of the tuple is additive — a count, a sum or a
maximum — so partial results can be merged in any order. That is what makes a \textbf{combiner}
valid here: each mapper merges its own tuples before the shuffle, and the reducer finishes the job
with exactly the same code.

This analysis consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Driver Class} — sets up the job, and registers the reducer as the combiner as well.
  \item \textbf{Mapper Class} — emits \code{app\_id} of type \code{Text} and a tuple of custom type
        \code{ReviewStatsTuple}.
  \item \textbf{Reducer Class} — merges all tuples of one game and emits \code{app\_id} with the
        final \code{ReviewStatsTuple}; also used as the combiner.
  \item \textbf{Tuple Class} — custom \code{Writable} holding reviews, recommended, total hours,
        maximum hours and helpful votes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Binning Games by Rating}
\textbf{Performs binning on the Steam rating label, writing every game into one output file per
bin.} The program is \code{BinningByRating}.

This is the \textbf{binning} pattern from the data organization family. The job is map-only: each
mapper decides which bin a record belongs to and writes it straight to that bin's file through
\code{MultipleOutputs}, so there is no shuffle, no sort and no reducer. The bin name comes from the
\code{rating} column with the spaces removed (``Very Positive'' becomes \code{VeryPositive-m-00000}),
and a counter per bin records how many games landed in it. \code{LazyOutputFormat} stops Hadoop from
also writing an empty default \code{part-m-00000} file.

This analysis consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Driver Class} — sets zero reducers and registers the \code{bins} named output.
  \item \textbf{Mapper Class} — uses \code{MultipleOutputs} to write each game to the file of its rating
        bin, and counts the games per bin.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Games Joined with their Reviews}
\textbf{Returns each game's title and rating together with how many people reviewed it and how many
hours they played it} (\code{GameReviewJoin}).

This is a \textbf{reduce-side join}. The two inputs have different layouts, so each gets its own
mapper through \code{MultipleInputs}. Both mappers use \code{app\_id} as the key and tag their values —
\code{G} for a game record, \code{R} for review data — so that the reducer, which receives
everything for one \code{app\_id} together, can tell the two sides apart and put them back together.

Sending all 41 million reviews through the shuffle one by one would be slow, so the review mapper
uses \textbf{in-mapper combining}: it keeps a running total per game in a hash map and emits a single
\code{R} record per game when its input split is done. The join is an inner join — games nobody
reviewed, and reviews whose game is missing from \code{games.csv}, are dropped and counted.

This analysis consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Driver Class} — registers the two inputs, each with its own mapper, and one reducer.
  \item \textbf{Game Mapper} — emits \code{app\_id} and \code{"G, title, rating"}.
  \item \textbf{Recommendation Mapper} — adds up reviews, recommendations and hours per game in memory
        and emits \code{app\_id} and \code{"R, reviews, recommended, hours"}.
  \item \textbf{Join Reducer} — matches the \code{G} record with the \code{R} records of the same game and
        emits the joined line.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Pig Analysis}

All five scripts read the dataset straight from HDFS with \code{PigStorage(',')} and drop the header row
with \code{FILTER \dots\ BY app\_id IS NOT NULL} — the header's \code{app\_id} is the text
``app\_id'', which fails the cast to \code{int} and becomes null. Results are stored tab separated
under \code{/steam/pig/}; a tab is used instead of a pipe because 104 game titles contain a
\code{|} character.

Every ranking sorts \emph{inside} a nested \code{FOREACH} block (\code{ORDER} then \code{LIMIT}) rather
than with a top-level \code{ORDER BY}. For the ``in each rating group'' queries this is what the
question needs — a separate top 10 per group — and for the overall rankings it avoids the
sampling job that a top-level \code{ORDER BY} starts.

Each run ends with a Pig warning, \code{FIELD\_DISCARDED\_TYPE\_CONVERSION\_FAILED}. It counts the
fields of the header rows that could not be cast to a number, and it is exactly how the header is
filtered out, so it is expected rather than a data problem.

\subsection{Top 10 Games by Total Hours Played}
\textbf{Returns the ten games whose reviewers played them for the most hours in total.}

This script reads the full 2 GB review table. It adds up the hours per game with
\code{GROUP BY app\_id} and \code{SUM}, then brings in each game's title and rating with a
\textbf{replicated join}: \code{games.csv} is small enough to be copied to every mapper, so the join
happens on the map side without a second shuffle. The hours stay numeric for the sort and are only
formatted with \code{SPRINTF} for the output.

Pig turns the script into a chain of three MapReduce jobs (visible in the Job Stats of the
screenshot below): a map-only job that prepares \code{games.csv} for the replicated join, one job with
15 maps and 3 reducers that groups the 41 million reviews and performs the join, and a final job
that ranks the result.

\section{What the Data Says}

\textbf{Steam reviews are mostly positive.} 85.8\% of the 41 million reviews recommend the game, and
71.7\% of all games carry one of the four positive labels. Only 2,226 games (4.4\%) carry a negative
one, and just 14 are Overwhelmingly Negative.

\textbf{Attention is concentrated in a few games.} Of the 50,872 games, 37,610 appear in the review
data at all, and half of those have 39 reviews or fewer. The ten most reviewed games — 0.03\% of the
reviewed games — hold 5.4\% of all reviews.

\textbf{The most played games are long-running multiplayer titles.} Team Fortress 2, Counter-Strike:
Global Offensive, Dota 2 and Rust each passed 90 million hours of play among their reviewers, and the
average Counter-Strike or Dota 2 reviewer had played about 429 hours. Across all reviews the players
had logged 4.14 billion hours.

\textbf{Popular does not mean well rated.} PUBG: BATTLEGROUNDS is the second most reviewed game on
Steam but is rated Mixed, with 57\% positive reviews; Overwatch® 2 has 181,198 reviews and is
Overwhelmingly Negative. At the other end, Wallpaper Engine is 98.2\% positive in the review data.

\textbf{A rating label is partly a volume label.} The Positive and Negative groups top out at 49
reviews, because Steam reserves the ``Very'' and ``Overwhelmingly'' labels for games with more reviews.
Grouping by \code{rating} therefore mixes two things — how good the reviews are and how many there are
— which is worth keeping in mind before comparing the groups directly.

\section{Conclusion}

A 2.2 GB, three-table Steam dataset was loaded into HDFS and analysed on a single-node Hadoop 3.4.1
cluster with three Java MapReduce programs and five Pig Latin scripts, all of which completed
successfully.

The three MapReduce programs each show a different design pattern. The summarization job shows how a
custom \code{Writable} and a combiner shrink 41 million map outputs to under a hundred thousand before
the shuffle. The binning job shows a map-only job writing many outputs at once. The reduce-side join
shows two differently shaped inputs meeting in one reducer, and how in-mapper combining makes the
join over 41 million rows finish in just over a minute.

Pig answered the ranking questions in 15 to 33 lines per script, including a replicated join over the
full review table, but took roughly three to four times as long as the hand-tuned Java join for the
same answer. The two tools agreed on every result they share, which is the strongest evidence that
both are correct.
